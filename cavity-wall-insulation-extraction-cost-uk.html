1. The Physics of Failure: Why Wet Cavity Beads Cause Devastating Damp

To understand why cavity insulation extraction has become one of the fastest-growing remedial trades in the UK, you have to understand why cavity walls were invented in the first place. From the late 1920s onwards, British builders moved away from solid brick construction. They introduced a continuous 50mm to 75mm air gap between the outer leaf of facing brickwork and the inner leaf of thermalite or concrete blockwork. This gap served one absolute engineering purpose: to create a capillary break. Rain hitting the external wall would soak into the porous brickwork, run harmlessly down the inside face of the outer leaf, and drain out through weep holes or ground-level subfloor air bricks.

During the government-subsidised energy efficiency drives of the 1990s and 2010s, millions of UK homes were retrofitted with cavity wall insulation (CWI). Technicians drilled grids of holes into outer masonry and pumped in loose expander polystyrene (EPS) beads, mineral fibre wool, or urea-formaldehyde foam. In suitable properties in sheltered geographic regions, this worked well. However, in millions of properties across high-exposure zones—particularly coastal parts of Wales, Scotland, South West England, and the North West—it turned into a disaster.

When wind-driven rain saturates external brickwork, water penetrates the outer leaf. If the cavity is packed tight with retrofitted material, capillary action draws water across the fill material directly into the inner leaf blockwork. Mortar "snots" (excess mortar dropped onto wall ties during original construction) accentuate the issue by trapping water against wet beads. The result is rapid thermal bridging, severe penetrating damp, blistering paint, ruined plaster, wet rot in floor joist tails, and dangerous black toxic mould (Stachybotrys chartarum).
